Male Pattern Baldness

Affects ~50% of men by age 50. The most common form of hair loss worldwide.

Androgenetic alopecia causes progressive thinning at the crown and temples. Driven by DHT sensitivity in the hair follicle, it follows a predictable pattern classified on the Norwood scale.

Receding temples Crown thinning Norwood scale Gradual onset

Female Pattern Hair Loss

Affects ~40% of women by age 70. Often misdiagnosed or dismissed.

Androgenetic alopecia in women presents as diffuse thinning across the crown rather than a receding hairline. Classified on the Ludwig scale. Hormonal changes — especially around menopause — can accelerate loss.

Diffuse thinning Crown widening Ludwig scale Postmenopausal

Am I losing hair?

Hair loss isn't always obvious at first. If you've noticed any of these signs, a free assessment can help identify what's happening and what your options are.

More hair than usual on your pillow or in the shower
Hairline that has moved back at the temples
A wider parting or visible scalp when your hair is wet
Sudden patchy loss or bald spots
Hair that feels noticeably thinner or finer than before

Education

What causes hair loss?

Most hair loss is caused by one or more of these four factors. Understanding which applies to you is the first step to choosing the right treatment.

🧬

Genetics

The most common cause. If your parents or grandparents experienced hair loss, you are significantly more likely to as well. The AR gene, which governs androgen receptor sensitivity, is the primary inherited factor.

Male pattern baldness (androgenetic alopecia)
Female pattern hair loss
Inherited alopecia universalis (rare)
See: Male Pattern Baldness · Female Pattern Hair Loss →
⚗️

Hormones

DHT (dihydrotestosterone) binds to follicle receptors, shrinking the hair growth cycle over time. Hormonal fluctuations — pregnancy, menopause, thyroid issues — can also trigger temporary or permanent shedding.

DHT sensitivity in androgenetic alopecia
Postpartum oestrogen drop
Hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism
See: Receding Hairline · Postpartum Hair Loss →
🧠

Stress & illness

Physical or emotional shock can push a large number of follicles into the telogen (resting) phase simultaneously, causing diffuse shedding 2–3 months after the triggering event.

Telogen effluvium (stress-induced shedding)
High fever, surgery, or major illness
Autoimmune triggers (alopecia areata)
See: Stress-Related Hair Loss · Alopecia Areata →
🥗

Nutrition & lifestyle

Hair follicles require iron, zinc, biotin, and adequate protein to complete a healthy growth cycle. Deficiencies — often from crash dieting or restrictive eating — produce temporary but significant shedding.

Iron deficiency anaemia
Crash dieting or low-protein diets
Traction from tight hairstyles
See: Traction Alopecia · Stress-Related Hair Loss →
Note: Most people experiencing hair loss have a combination of factors at play. A clinical assessment — whether in-app or with a verified provider — is the most accurate way to identify the root cause and the best-matched treatment.
